Therapy+ · Working Sheet · Session 03

Message

Confused people do not buy. Resonance over persuasion.
Four sections. Everything you write later — your website copy, your emails, your outreach, the words inside your own program — starts here, so keep this page and keep adding to it. In section two, aim for ten lines under each heading and add rows until you run out. Do not edit while you write.

01High level, and your coaching statement

Start wide, then narrow to one line. Who do you want to help, what is their problem, and what changes for them? Then what they need to feel before they would act. Then say all of it in one sentence — the one you use when somebody asks what you do. One sentence, not three.
Example. Hero: a high-performing executive whose perfectionism keeps her permanently burnt out. Problem: nothing is ever good enough to let go of, so she works late, sleeps badly and is on edge with everybody. Result: she performs at the same level without the exhaustion. Before she acts she needs to feel that easing off is not the same as slipping.
Example. I work with senior executives whose perfectionism is burning them out, so that they can perform at the level they expect of themselves without living on the edge of exhaustion — and without lowering the standard they are known for.
Example. What do you do? I help senior executives ship work at a pace they can sustain, without dropping the standard they are known for. Who is it for? Leaders three to five years into a bigger job than the one they were hired for. How is this different from therapy? Therapy works on what is in the way. Coaching works on where you are going — structured, goal-led work with an end point. If something clinical needs attention I will say so and help you find the right person.

02What is true for them

You are not writing copy yet — you are working out what is going on. Name it under each heading, in plain terms; the wording comes later, out of these. Five things, and the first of them has three levels. Aim for ten under each. Put everything down before you judge any of it.
A · External problems — what tangible problems or challenges is the hero facing?
Example. Missing deadlines because nothing is ever signed off. Re-doing work three or four times. Fourteen-hour days. Cancelling things she had looked forward to. Not sleeping the night before anything is due.
External problems — what tangible problems or challenges is the hero facing?
B · Internal problems — how does the external problem make the hero feel on a personal level?
Example. Feels like a fraud despite the title. Believes she is only as good as her last piece of work. Ashamed of how much it takes out of her. Constantly on edge, and short with people she cares about.
Internal problems — how does the external problem make the hero feel on a personal level?
C · Philosophical problems — what bigger picture or ethical issues are tied to the hero’s problem?
Example. It should not cost your health to do a job well. Nobody should have to choose between doing good work and having a life. Exhaustion has been dressed up as commitment for a generation.
Philosophical problems — what bigger picture or ethical issues are tied to the hero’s problem?
D · Desired outcomes — specific enough to picture an ordinary Tuesday inside it
Example. Finishing at a reasonable hour without guilt. Signing work off first time. Sleeping through the night before a deadline. Being someone her team finds calm to be around.
Desired outcomes — specific enough to picture an ordinary Tuesday inside it
E · Obstacles — what’s stopping them from improving their own life?
Example. Has delegated and taken it back twice. Believes the standard IS her, so easing off feels like becoming someone worse. No idea what good enough looks like. No time to work it out.
Obstacles — what’s stopping them from improving their own life?
F · Cost of inaction — what does it cost them three months, six months, a year, five years?
Example. Three months: another quarter as the bottleneck. Six months: the team routes around her. A year: burnout leave, or a resignation she did not plan. Five years: the health thing she keeps postponing.
Cost of inaction — what does it cost them three months, six months, a year, five years?
G · Authority and trust — your training, your years, your results, and what others say about you
Example. Fifteen years as a licensed therapist. Several hundred hours with high performers in exactly this position. Work that used to sit for weeks goes out on time, without the standard slipping.
Authority and trust — your training, your years, your results, and what others say about you
Ten minutes · the one that tests the rest

03The story

Write it as a short story about a hypothetical client you'd work with — one you have invented, not one you have met. Where they are now, what they badly want, what is in the way, the moment they meet a guide — that is you — who offers a way through, and where they end up. It is the fastest way to hear whether the two sections above actually hang together. If the story limps, something further up is still vague.
Example. Maya runs a division of four hundred people and she is genuinely excellent at it — which is what makes this hard to see, and harder to say out loud. She is also awake at five re-reading work she has already re-read, short with her team by four, and home too late to hear about anybody’s day. She has canceled the same weekend away twice. Somewhere in the last two years she started measuring herself only by the last thing she shipped, and the sense of running out of time never quite lifts. She wants to be this good without it costing her everything. She has delegated and clawed it back, read two books on it, and quietly decided the standard is just who she is. Then she starts working with a coach who has sat with high performers for years, who gives her a way to tell finished from perfect and a rule for when to send. It is uncomfortable for a couple of weeks. By the fall the work is no worse, she is sleeping, and she was at the table for her daughter’s birthday.

04Your outreach

One announcement, then two follow-ups. Start them here — you finish them on The List tab, which is where they live and where you update them as you learn what gets answered. Write all three while the words are fresh, so sending them later takes no decision. Use your own energy, style and voice. It has to sound like you.
Example. Some news. I have been a therapist for fifteen years and I love what I do. Alongside that, I have just launched something new — a coaching program for senior leaders whose perfectionism is quietly costing them their evenings, their sleep and their patience. They are excellent at the job; that is rather the problem. Do you know anyone who might be interested in this?
Example. “Hi Sarah — just following up on my note last week. Did anyone come to mind?”
Example. “Hi Sarah — I know how busy you are, so I’ll leave this one with you. If somebody springs to mind down the line, reach out any time.”

Story terms

The shape of this session comes from Donald Miller's Building a StoryBrand. You never have to use the words — but knowing which piece you are writing is what stops one message trying to do two jobs badly.
The heroThe one person you are writing to — your client, never your business. They do the walking; everything is written to them
The guideYou. Somebody who has been down this road before, who hands them a plan and a reason to believe it is possible, then steps back
The villainThe thing standing in their way. Make it one thing, make it real, and remember it works on three levels rather than one
External problemWhat is factually going wrong — the nuts and bolts of it. Most writing never gets past this level
Internal problemHow that problem makes them feel about themselves. Usually some version of “do I even have what it takes?” — and this is the level that makes somebody feel understood
Philosophical problemWhy it is simply not right that anybody should have to put up with this. The level that makes it matter rather than just annoy
The promised landTheir desired outcome — the new normal once they have what they want. Not the absence of the problem, but the ordinary day they get to live instead
The planThe vehicle that carries them from where they are now to the promised land. That is your coaching program. You build it in Session 04; here you only need to know it sits between the two
StakesWhat it costs them if nothing changes. Too little and the message is bland, too much and you have lost them — the setting is honest, not frightening
AuthorityWhy you are credible on exactly this. Your training, your years, and the results you have already achieved for people
Social proofWhat other people have said about you. You will have little of this at first, and that is normal
Call to actionWhat you ask them to do next. Your announcement is the gentle kind: news, ending in a question anybody can answer
